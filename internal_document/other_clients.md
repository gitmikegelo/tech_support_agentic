## First — Reframe What "Agentic" Means Commercially

Most people equate agentic with "AI chatbot that uses tools." That's one shape. The actual pattern is broader:

> **Agentic = software that pursues a goal across multiple steps, uses tools/systems autonomously, reasons about what to do next, and handles branching logic that's too complex for deterministic workflows.**

Once you see it this way, agentic solutions fit **anywhere there's an expert human doing a multi-step investigative or judgment-heavy task inside a tool-rich environment.** That's a huge surface area.

Three commercial shapes to keep in mind:
1. **Copilot** — human stays in the loop, agent augments. (Lowest risk, fastest sale.)
2. **Autopilot with approval gates** — agent executes, human approves at key moments. (Medium risk, high ROI.)
3. **Full autonomy** — agent runs end-to-end, only escalates exceptions. (Highest risk, highest value.)

Most enterprise sales land at #1 or #2. #3 is the 3-year roadmap.

## What Makes an Opportunity Actually Sellable (Filter)

Use this filter on any idea:

1. **Is there a senior human doing repetitive investigation today?** If yes → candidate.
2. **Are the tools/data digital and API-accessible?** If no → not yet.
3. **Are outcomes measurable?** (AHT, throughput, error rate, hours saved.) If no → hard to sell.
4. **Are mistakes recoverable or gated?** If no → needs heavy compliance work.
5. **Is there existing organizational urgency?** (Budget, mandate, fear.) If no → education sale, longer cycle.
6. **Can you show value in 6–12 weeks?** If no → doesn't fit Accenture's pilot model.

The best opportunities score yes on all six. Your telco copilot does.

## The Meta-Play: Build a Portfolio, Not a Product

Here's the strategic insight most people miss:

> You shouldn't pick *one* vertical and build deeper. You should build **a portfolio of 3–4 reference copilots across different industries** that all share the same underlying agentic architecture.

Why:
1. You demonstrate **architectural generality** — "same core, different tools" is the most compelling enterprise AI story right now.
2. You become the **go-to agentic expert in your practice** across deals, not just in one vertical.
3. Each reference asset costs you maybe 2–3 weeks to produce once you have the core. ROI is enormous.
4. Accenture partners in different verticals will pull you into their deals.

A concrete portfolio I'd build in your position:
1. **Telco support copilot** ✅ (done)
2. **SecOps Tier 1 analyst copilot** (horizontal, sells everywhere, high demand)
3. **Financial crime / KYC investigation copilot** (banking, regulated, high-value)
4. **Healthcare prior auth copilot** (massive market, distinctive)

Four assets, shared core, covers maybe 60% of Accenture's biggest verticals.

## One Honest Warning

Every Big 4 consultancy and every systems integrator is pitching "agentic AI" right now. **Differentiation is not the agent — everyone has one.** Differentiation is:

1. **Domain fidelity** — does it actually reflect how the work is done?
2. **Integration depth** — does it plug into real client systems, not mocks?
3. **Risk handling** — approval gates, citations, audit trails, graceful degradation.
4. **Evaluation rigor** — can you prove it works on real data?
5. **Deployment muscle** — can Accenture actually ship it at scale?

Your POC nails #3. Partially nails #1. Needs work on #2, #4, #5 — which is exactly what a client engagement pays for.

**Don't compete on "we have agents." Compete on "we have agents that survive your compliance team, your ops team, and your CIO." That's a much shorter list.**

## #1 — The Controls & Compliance Evidence Agent
**Industry**: Any regulated enterprise (banks, insurers, healthcare, pharma, utilities)
**Novelty**: 8/10 • **Feasibility**: 9/10 • **Sellability**: 10/10

### The problem they don't think they have
Every enterprise runs dozens of compliance control programs — SOX, SOC 2, HIPAA, PCI, ISO 27001, model risk (SR 11-7), GDPR, etc. **Evidence collection** consumes armies of GRC analysts who spend 60% of their time screenshotting, exporting logs, writing "tested and passed" memos, and routing them to auditors.

Nobody has framed this as an agentic problem. Everyone treats it as a GRC platform problem (Archer, ServiceNow GRC) — but those platforms are workflow tools, not intelligent collectors.

### What the agent does
Given a control ("All production database access is logged and reviewed monthly"), the agent:
1. Identifies the systems to query (via a control-to-system mapping).
2. Calls APIs to pull evidence (AWS CloudTrail, Okta, Splunk, database audit logs).
3. Validates completeness against the control language.
4. Drafts the testing memo with citations.
5. Routes to human for approval.

### Why it sells
- **Every F500 has this pain.** Universal.
- **ROI is concrete**: 40–70% reduction in evidence-collection hours.
- **Audit-defensible by design** — citations + trace logs are the point, not a feature.
- **CISO, Chief Compliance Officer, CFO** all want this. Three buyers.
- **Repeatable across every client.** Near-zero customization for the core; just add connectors.

### Why it's feasible for you
Your citation enforcement + risk tiers + observability design is **purpose-built for this use case**. You'd build it faster than anyone else approaching it fresh.

## #2 — The Alert Fatigue Triage Agent (SecOps / Tier-1 SOC)
**Industry**: Any enterprise with a SOC — financial services, healthcare, critical infrastructure
**Novelty**: 7/10 • **Feasibility**: 9/10 • **Sellability**: 10/10

### The problem
SOC analysts drown in 5,000–50,000 alerts/day. 95%+ are false positives. Analysts burn out, real threats get missed, and MDR vendors charge a fortune for what is effectively triage labor.

"AI SOC" startups (Dropzone, Prophet, Radiant) are proving this market hard — which is exactly why Accenture clients want to consider it without buying a startup they can't trust yet.

### What the agent does
For each alert:
1. Enriches from SIEM, EDR, threat intel, identity provider, asset DB.
2. Runs an investigation plan (same pattern as your telco copilot — hypothesis → tool calls → evidence).
3. Classifies: false positive (auto-close with evidence), benign (deprioritize), suspicious (escalate with pre-built case).
4. Writes the investigation narrative analysts would have written.

### Why it sells
- **Board-level problem.** CISOs are personally accountable.
- **Huge budget.** Security teams spend $10M+/year on MDR services; this displaces a chunk.
- **Measurable in weeks**: alerts closed per hour, MTTR, analyst hours recovered.
- **Accenture has deep SecOps practices** (Accenture Security is a multi-billion-dollar business).
- **Every industry buys it.** Horizontal play.

### Why it's feasible
Your architecture is literally shaped like a SOC investigator: evidence log + multi-step diagnosis + citation + approval for containment actions. Swap tools, repurpose the loop.

### The strategic kicker
This is the **second asset I'd build after telco.** Maximum reach inside Accenture — every industry team pulls you into their deals.

## #3 — The Shadow IT & SaaS Sprawl Investigator
**Industry**: Horizontal (every CIO/CISO has this; enterprise-wide buyer)
**Novelty**: 9/10 • **Feasibility**: 8/10 • **Sellability**: 8/10

- Compliance risk (HIPAA data in unapproved tools)

SaaS management platforms (Zylo, Productiv, Torii) find the apps. **Nobody uses agents to investigate and act on them.**

### What the agent does

2. For each unknown app: researches what it is, who uses it, what data it touches.
3. Cross-references against approved vendor list, contracts, security reviews.
4. Drafts a disposition: consolidate, approve, block, renegotiate.
5. For high-risk: opens a ticket to the right owner with evidence.

### Why it sells
- **CFO cares** (spend waste). **CISO cares** (data exfil). **CIO cares** (architecture sanity).
- **Hard-dollar savings story** — typical F500 can reclaim $5M–$30M/year on SaaS spend alone.
- **Very low competition** — the category is "SaaS management," but nobody is running agents over the data.

### Why it's feasible
Tools are all API-accessible (Okta, Expensify, NetSkope, DNS logs). Mock integrations work fine for the demo.

### The novelty angle
This is a problem every client **has but hasn't named.** Walking into a CIO's office with "did you know you have 847 SaaS apps and here's what 312 of them do" is a conversation-stopper. That's what makes it sellable.

### Why it sells

### Why it's feasible (mostly)

### Why the novelty score is high
Most legal AI is **document-by-document analysis**. This is **thesis-driven investigation**. Different paradigm.

## #6 — The Regulatory Change Impact Agent

## #7 — The "Silent Failure" Detection Agent for Enterprise Data Pipelines
**Industry**: Any data-mature enterprise (banking, retail, healthcare, tech)
**Novelty**: 10/10 • **Feasibility**: 8/10 • **Sellability**: 8/10

### The problem nobody is talking about
Modern data stacks (dbt, Snowflake, Airflow, Databricks) have **observability tools** for pipeline failures. But **silent failures** — where pipelines succeed but produce wrong data — are far more dangerous. Schema drift, upstream changes, logic errors, partial loads. Teams find out weeks later when an executive notices numbers look off.

Observability tools (Monte Carlo, Bigeye, Anomalo) detect statistical anomalies. **They don't investigate root cause.**

### What the agent does
When an anomaly is flagged (or proactively, on a schedule):
1. Investigates: "Revenue for region X dropped 30% day-over-day. Why?"
2. Traces lineage upstream.
3. Checks recent schema changes, dbt model changes, source system changes.
4. Runs diagnostic queries.
5. Drafts a root cause memo: "Source system changed currency column from USD to local currency on Tuesday. Affects 14 downstream models."
6. Proposes the fix.

### Why it sells
- **Data quality is finally a board-level issue** because it breaks AI initiatives.
- **Chief Data Officer is a real buyer with real budget** now.
- **Very few people are solving this.** Mostly startups.
- **Accenture has a huge data practice.** Natural fit.

### Why the novelty is maxed out
The pattern "agent that investigates its own data infrastructure" is genuinely fresh. Clients light up when they see it.

### Why it's feasible
Lineage tools, dbt metadata, warehouse query logs — all API-accessible. Agent reasoning over structured metadata is easy.

## #9 — The Clinical Documentation & Coding Integrity Agent
**Industry**: Healthcare providers (hospitals, health systems)
**Novelty**: 8/10 • **Feasibility**: 7/10 • **Sellability**: 10/10

### The problem
Hospitals lose billions annually to **under-coded** claims (services delivered but not billed correctly) and **denied claims** (billing errors caught by payers). Coding companies (3M, Optum) do this with humans + rules-based tools. Startups (Fathom, Nym) do autonomous coding.

**The agentic angle no one has cracked**: clinical documentation *integrity*. Reading a clinician's note and asking "does this note adequately support the code that was billed?" — and if not, drafting the clarifying query to send back to the clinician.

### What the agent does
1. Reads clinical notes for an encounter.
2. Reviews assigned codes.

## #10 — The Internal Knowledge Investigator (for Complex Enterprises)
**Industry**: Horizontal — any company >10,000 employees
**Novelty**: 9/10 • **Feasibility**: 8/10 • **Sellability**: 8/10

### The problem they don't know they have
Large enterprises have knowledge fragmented across SharePoint, Confluence, Notion, Slack, Google Drive, email, Jira, service desk tickets, meeting recordings, network drives. Enterprise search (Glean, Guru, Coveo) retrieves documents. **Nobody investigates across them to answer an actual question.**

Typical employee question: *"What's our policy on promoting an employee whose manager is on PIP, in a region that's in a hiring freeze, and the employee is on an H1B?"* — the answer exists across 6 documents, 2 Slack threads, and a 2023 email that nobody can find.

### What the agent does
1. Understands the question (multi-condition queries).
2. Plans an investigation — which sources likely have what part of the answer.
3. Executes searches, reads results, identifies gaps.
4. Cross-references conflicting sources (which one is authoritative, which is stale).
5. Synthesizes with citations and flags uncertainties.

### Why it sells
- **CIO budget for "enterprise AI search" is real and growing.**
- **Every employee is a user.** Scales enormously.
- **Measurable**: time-to-answer, ticket deflection, repeat-question reduction.
- **Differentiates from Glean** — which is retrieval, not investigation.

### Why the novelty angle matters
The moment you show a demo where the agent reasons across 6 different sources to answer one question, with citations, you've destroyed every "enterprise search" competitor in the room.

### Why feasibility is 8/10
RAG + source connectors + agentic loop. You have everything you need. Each client needs connector work, but Bedrock + LlamaIndex/similar covers the basics.
